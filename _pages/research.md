## Job Market Paper

<div class="paper">
  <div class="paper-title"><a href="/files/JMP_YingXu.pdf"> Navigating Competitive Pressures: School Responses to Competition and the Distributional Effects on Student Achievement
</a></div>
  <div class="paper-abstract"> This paper examines the equity-efficiency implications of traditional public schools' (TPSs) responses to increased competition from charter schools. Using administrative data from North Carolina, I find that TPSs respond to student and teacher attrition to charter schools by reducing the number of classes, resulting in increased class sizes and higher student-teacher ratios. To maintain school proficiency rates, TPSs strategically reassign high-quality teachers to low-achieving and economically disadvantaged students. These effects remain consistent even after accounting for changes in school composition. Furthermore, I develop and estimate an empirical model of school choice and competition to analyze the impact of charter sector expansion. The findings indicate that expanding charter schools promotes a more equitable distribution of high-quality teachers within TPSs. Additionally, optimizing teacher-student matching emerges as an effective strategy to improve allocative efficiency.</div>
</div>

## Working Papers

<div class="paper">
  <div class="paper-title"><a href="/files/School_Closure.pdf"> Wildfire Exposure and Learning Loss: Evidence from California </a></div>
  <div class="paper-meta">with <a href="https://coauthor-website.com">Yifeng Luo</a></div>
<div class="paper-funding">Funded by the Russell Sage Foundation and William T. Grant Foundation for Improving Education Equality.</div> 
  <details class="paper-details">
    <summary>Abstract</summary>
    <div class="paper-abstract"> Wildfires have grown larger, more frequent, and more geographically widespread in the past decade, disrupting schooling and student learning. This paper links California student assessment data to detailed information on wildfires, school closures, wildfire smoke, and weather conditions to examine the causal effects of wildfire exposure on student academic achievement and the mechanisms through which these effects operate. Leveraging exogenous variation in wildfire shocks across time, geography, and intensity across schools and cohorts, we find that exposure to wildfires decreases Math and ELA scores by about 0.028 and 0.020 standard deviations, with larger effects among students with lower socioeconomic backgrounds. We also exploit quasi-random variation in wind direction and wildfire characteristics as instruments for school closures and smoke exposure to explore potential mechanisms. Our estimates identify both wildfire-induced school closures and smoke-related air pollution as important mechanisms: benchmark calculations suggest that closures account for roughly 15–20\% of the observed learning losses, while smoke exposure accounts for an additional 33\% of the decline in math achievement and 5\% of the decline in ELA achievement.</div>
  </details>
</div>

<div class="paper">
  <div class="paper-title">Take-up and Targeting</div>
<div class="paper-status">Draft coming soon</div>
<div class="paper-meta">with <a href="https://sites.google.com/view/mdeshpande"> Manasi Deshpande</a> and <a href="https://www.charlierafkin.com">Charlie Rafkin</a></div>
  <details class="paper-details">
    <summary>Abstract</summary>
    <div class="paper-abstract"> What are the impacts of enrollment hassles, or ``ordeals,'' on the take-up and targeting of transfer programs? A large literature studies this question, but it is hard to interpret because the research examines different settings and measures of need. We perform a meta-analysis of 130 studies and 570 targeting estimates, converting each study's estimates into a common measure of targeting: the gap in expected consumption between marginal enrollees and existing enrollees. On average, ordeals reduce take-up but have zero targeting effects. Targeting does not vary predictably with the program's baseline hassle level, recipients' average need, the type of hassle, or other study characteristics. We develop a framework to study the normative properties of ordeals in terms of gains to marginal and inframarginal enrollees. When calibrated using the meta-analysis, the welfare benefits of ordeals in terms of targeting are typically small in magnitude. Because ordeals also impose deadweight loss on enrollees, reducing them would likely raise welfare.</div>
  </details>
</div>

## Work in Progress
<div class="paper">
  <div class="paper-title">The Value of Work and the Social Safety Net</a></div>
<div class="paper-meta">with <a href="https://sites.google.com/view/mdeshpande"> Manasi Deshpande</a> and <a href="https://www.charlierafkin.com">Rebecca Dizon-Ross</a></div>
  <details class="paper-details">
    <summary>Abstract</summary>
    <div class="paper-abstract"> This study will examine how veterans make decisions about whether to work, what kind of work to do, and what features of jobs matter to them, with particular attention to veterans with service-connected health conditions and those receiving government benefits. The study has three specific aims. First, the study will examine financial and non-financial motivations for work and volunteer participation among veterans by collecting survey data from veterans about their preferences over job features. We aim to understand what drives this participation, including factors such as maintaining daily routine, building social connections, developing independence, contributing to meaningful activities, and gaining a sense of purpose or accomplishment. Second, the study will identify job features and workplace arrangements that make employment more feasible for veterans. We will examine whether features such as flexible scheduling, remote work, supportive environments, meaningful tasks, or skill development opportunities affect veterans' willingness and ability to work. Third, the study aims to understand how health status and benefit receipt affect motivations and preferences for work.</div>
  </details>
</div>


<div class="paper">
  <div class="paper-title">The Impact of Adverse Weather and Holidays on Hospital Visit Timing and Eye Condition Severity</a></div>
<div class="paper-meta">with <a href="https://sites.google.com/view/mdeshpande"> Manasi Deshpande</a>, <a href=" https://www.london.edu/faculty-and-research/faculty-profiles/r/ramdas-k ">Kamalini Ramdas</a>, <a href="https://www.charlierafkin.com">Sanjay Jain</a>, Josephine Christy, and Rengaraj Venkatesh </div>
  <details class="paper-details">
    <summary>Abstract</summary>
    <div class="paper-abstract"> This study examines how delays in hospital visits after eye injury affect patients’ eye condition severity and longer-term multidimensional outcomes, including health outcomes, and social outcomes. Timely treatment is especially important for eye conditions because delays may worsen visual acuity, increase the risk of complications, and reduce the likelihood of full recovery. Understanding the consequences of delayed care is therefore important for both clinical practice and health policy, particularly in settings where patients face substantial barriers to accessing specialized care. To identify the causal effect of delay in hospital visits, this study exploits plausibly exogenous variation in delay caused by adverse weather, holidays, and elections. In particular, the study incorporates rich weather data along the route from each patient’s village to the Aravind eye hospital Pondicherry, and the data on local holidays and election activities. This allows us to measure whether patients were exposed to adverse weather conditions or holidays/elections during the period immediately after injury.</div>
  </details>
</div>

<div class="paper">
  <div class="paper-title">Incentives to Finance: How Governments Affect the Tuition-Free Policy in Vocational Schools in China</div>
  <div class="paper-meta">with Zhilei Tian</div>
  <details class="paper-details">
    <summary>Abstract</summary>
    <div class="paper-abstract"> This paper assesses how government subsidies, aimed at reducing schooling costs to increase enrollment, interact with the supply-side fiscal burden. Policies like school fee waivers may heighten fiscal pressure on governments and potentially dampen their motivation to fund education, especially if such policies are imposed by higher-level authorities rather than being self-initiated. We analyze the impact of fiscal burdens from tuition-free policies on enrollment in China’s secondary vocational schools. Through a geographic regression discontinuity design exploiting variation in fiscal responsibilities across county borders, we find a 21% enrollment decrease in these schools, with variations ranging from 14% to 40%. We also find that larger fiscal responsibilities borne by local governments lead to greater enrollment reductions. </div>
  </details>
